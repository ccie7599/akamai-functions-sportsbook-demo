    Overview

    One request path, one Function

    The business problem

    A sportsbook's busiest moments are unpredictable and brutal: kickoff, a goal, a promo going viral. Every one of those requests needs logic (who is this, where are they, what can they see, is this bet safe) before it touches the core platform. Today that logic lives in regional servers that have to be sized for the peak.

    This demo puts that logic on the request path instead. A single WebAssembly component runs on Akamai Functions and handles every route: the page you are looking at, redirects, personalization, bot handling, signed quotes and pre-trade risk scoring. Each chapter starts with the business problem, shows it running live, then confirms the value with a number measured on the spot.

    Chapter 1 Use case: Mass redirects

    The bettor arrives through one of 100,000 links

    The business problem

    Customers arrive through podcast codes, affiliate links, QR codes on TV and printed ads, old bookmarked URLs, and a front door that has to send them to the right state's site. Each one is a redirect. On a big weekend that is millions of requests that do nothing but look up a destination, and none of them should cost an origin round trip.

    The Function holds a 100,000-entry campaign table compiled into the component, plus legacy URL rules and jurisdiction routing. A lookup is a binary search in memory. Offers that differ by state get a state-specific landing page automatically.

    Chapter 2 Use case: Hyper-personalization
    
    
    Every bettor sees their own sportsbook
    
    
    The business problem
    
      
    A new customer, a VIP and someone who hasn't bet in two months should not see the same page. Their favorite team belongs at the top, offers depend on segment and on what their state allows, and a boosted price has to be sized to live risk: a boost is a price the book chooses to give away, and giving it away on a price that moved five points ten seconds ago is how a promotion turns into a liability.
    
    
    On every request the Function builds this customer's board from the book: favorite team first, promotions filtered by segment and jurisdiction, and a profit boost that trims itself when the featured price is moving and pauses while the market is suspended. Nothing is cached per user, so there is nothing to invalidate when prices move.

    Chapter 3 Use case: Bot misdirect
    
    
    Scrapers get prices that look right and aren't
    
    
    The business problem
    
      
    Odds screens, arbitrage bots and competitors scrape prices around the clock. Blocking them tells them to rotate IPs and try again. What costs them is data that looks perfectly normal and is quietly wrong, so their arbitrage math loses money and nothing tells them they've been caught.
    
    
    When a request is classified as automated, the Function serves the same board with every price shifted by a small, stable amount, some lines nudged half a point, and suspended markets shown as open. The noise is keyed to the scraper's fingerprint and a 30-second window, so repeated requests agree and can't be averaged out. In production the verdict comes from Akamai's bot detection; this demo uses simple signals so you can reproduce it with curl.

    Chapter 4 Use case: Token management
    
    
    Every quote is signed, and verified before it goes anywhere
    
    
    The business problem
    
      
    A bet slip carries a price. If the client can edit that price, or replay a quote from a minute ago after the line moved, the book pays for it. The quote needs to be issued and checked where the request arrives, in microseconds, without a round trip to the core.
    
    
    When a customer picks a price, the Function signs exactly what was quoted (selection, price, price version, 30-second expiry) with HMAC-SHA256. The pre-check trusts only what the signature covers. The same pattern works for stream tickets, session tokens and signed URLs.

    Chapter 5 Use case: AI inference
    
    
    Every bet is scored by the operator's own model before it leaves the edge
    
    
    The business problem
    
      
    Live bets usually carry a fixed acceptance delay to protect the book from bettors who see the play before the price moves. Every customer pays that delay, including the vast majority who are harmless. Scoring each slip as it arrives lets harmless bets through immediately and saves the delay, and the trader's attention, for the ones that need it.
    
    
    The pre-check verifies the signed quote, rejects stale prices, suspended markets and over-limit stakes, then scores the slip with a small model: stake relative to the bettor's normal, sharpness, quote age, in-play, betting velocity. The model here is a stand-in loaded from a JSON weights file; in production it is the operator's own tuned open-weight model. Acceptance still happens on the regulated platform; the ticket just arrives already scored.

    The business problem
    
      
    New features usually mean new services: something to deploy, scale, patch and pay for at peak. The question is how much of the application can live on the request path as a single deployable unit, with no servers behind it.

    Chapter 7 Platform: cold starts
    
    
    Zero penalty for cold traffic
    
    
    The business problem
    
      
    Serverless platforms usually keep warm instances around and charge a start-up delay when traffic arrives that no warm instance can take: the first requests after a deploy, a quiet region waking up, a spike beyond what's warm. On a game day, those are exactly the requests that matter.
    
    
    Akamai Functions creates a fresh WebAssembly sandbox for every request. The component keeps a request counter in memory; a counter that starts at zero in every new instance and reads 1 on every response means every request you send ran in a brand-new instance. If starting an instance cost anything noticeable, every request would pay it. Send a burst and look.

    Chapter 8 Architecture: real-time transport
    
    
    Functions handle the request; a stream tier holds the connection
    
    
    The business problem
    
      
    Live odds reach customers over WebSocket and Server-Sent Events: long-lived connections that stay open for the whole game. By default a CDN opens one origin connection for every client connection, so the servers holding those streams carry every open socket.
    
    
    Functions are request and response. They decide who may connect and what they may see: the signed-token pattern from chapter 4 becomes a stream ticket. A stream tier on Akamai cloud, one on-net hop from the CDN edge, holds the long-lived connections and carries the bytes. This demo refreshes its board by polling once a second, which is a legitimate pattern for a one-second board; the stream tier is what you add for tick-by-tick updates.
